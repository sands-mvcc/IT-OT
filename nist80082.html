01

Foundations

NIST Special Publication 800-82, Guide to Operational Technology (OT) Security, is federal guidance from NIST's Computer Security Resource Center (CSRC). It's free, vendor-neutral, and comprehensive enough that it's often the first document handed to a new OT security hire — but it is guidance, not a certifiable standard or a legal mandate.

Rev. 1 (2006) and Rev. 2 (2015) were both titled around Industrial Control Systems (ICS) Security — the classic SCADA/DCS/PLC world. Rev. 3 (2023) is a substantial rewrite: the title itself changed to Operational Technology (OT) Security, explicitly folding in building automation systems, physical access control systems, and other non-industrial OT. It also added SP 800-53 Rev. 5 overlays and tightened alignment with the NIST Cybersecurity Framework — both new to this revision.

It's the default reference for U.S. federal agencies operating OT-adjacent systems, where it feeds into FISMA-driven Risk Management Framework processes. Far beyond federal use, it's voluntarily adopted across manufacturing, water, energy, and healthcare facilities precisely because it costs nothing and doesn't require joining any certification scheme.

IEC 62443 is written by control-systems engineers around zones, conduits, and security levels. SP 800-82 is written inside NIST's broader risk-management ecosystem — it inherits the control catalog (SP 800-53), the risk process (SP 800-37), and the outcome language (the Cybersecurity Framework) that IT security teams already use, then tailors all three for OT. That heritage is exactly why it's often the easier on-ramp for an organization that already runs NIST-based IT security.

03

Positioning: SP 800-82 vs. the Other Core Frameworks

IEC 62443 is a standalone, certifiable design standard. ATT&CK for ICS is a descriptive threat-intelligence knowledge base. CIP-008 is a mandatory sector regulation. SP 800-82 sits in a fourth position: voluntary federal guidance that pulls an organization's OT risk management into the same ecosystem as its IT risk management.

Structure & Overlays

SP 800-82 Rev. 3 reads as four pillars building on each other: understand what OT actually is, manage risk against it, architect a defense, and align that defense to the broader NIST control ecosystem. The colors below deliberately reuse this site's existing strand colors — Understand borrows the orange used for "Foundations," Manage Risk borrows the purple used for "Frameworks," Architect borrows the blue used for "Protocols," and Align borrows the green used for "Operations."

Pillar 1 — Understand OT

Rev. 3 explicitly folds in building automation systems (HVAC, elevators, fire/life-safety), physical access control systems, and other non-industrial OT alongside traditional SCADA, DCS, and PLC environments — matching how converged campuses and facilities actually look today, not just process plants.

  • Safety over confidentiality — a compromised OT system can hurt someone; that reorders the priority the CIA triad usually assumes
  • Availability and determinism — a control loop needs a response within a fixed time window, every time
  • Long lifecycles — 15–20 year equipment lifespans mean legacy protocols and unpatched firmware are the norm, not the exception
  • Narrow patch windows — many systems can only be updated during scheduled outages, sometimes years apart

SCADA and DCS platforms, PLCs and RTUs, safety instrumented systems, building automation systems (BAS), physical access control systems (PACS), and the engineering workstations, HMIs, and historians that support them all fall inside Rev. 3's scope.

Pillar 2 — Manage Risk

A dedicated chapter walks through framing risk in OT terms, identifying assets and their criticality to safety and production, assessing risk with availability weighted above confidentiality, responding with controls that respect operational constraints, and monitoring continuously rather than through periodic-only reviews.

Nation-state and criminal actors, insiders and contractors, ransomware crossing over from a compromised IT network, and non-adversarial sources — equipment failure, natural disaster, or simple human error — that matter just as much in an availability-first environment.

Legacy protocols with no built-in authentication, flat unsegmented networks, default or shared credentials, and firmware that can't be patched without a planned outage — the same vulnerability classes Lesson 7 already covers, here framed as risk inputs rather than standalone findings.

Pillar 3 — Architect Defense

Layered controls across the network, host, and application levels, illustrated with example architectures that show where each layer sits relative to the Purdue model's levels.

SP 800-82 reinforces network segmentation and an IT/OT DMZ without inventing a competing vocabulary — it is written to be compatible with, and is frequently cited alongside, IEC 62443's zones-and-conduits model rather than replacing it.

Guidance on jump hosts, one-way data diodes, and tightly scoped vendor remote-access patterns — the architectural controls that show up again in Lesson 10's identity and access content.

Pillar 4 — Align to Frameworks & Controls

OT risk activities are mapped onto the CSF's functions — Govern, Identify, Protect, Detect, Respond, Recover in CSF 2.0 — giving an organization already using the CSF for IT a structured on-ramp for OT instead of a second, disconnected framework.

The headline new artifact in Rev. 3. An overlay is a pre-tailored subset of SP 800-53 controls, with OT-specific supplemental guidance, built for each impact tier (Low, Moderate, High). Instead of starting from the full IT-oriented control catalog, an OT team starts from a baseline already adjusted for legacy hardware, narrow patch windows, and safety priority.

Teams select the applicable overlay, then further tailor individual controls to their environment. Where a control genuinely isn't feasible — patching a 15-year-old HMI, for instance — the expected response is a documented compensating control and a POA&M entry, not silence. This is the direct bridge into Lesson 9's governance content.

05

Definitions & Scope

These terms come directly from the broader NIST risk-management vocabulary that SP 800-82 inherits and tailors.

Operational Technology (OT)

Hardware and software that detects or causes changes in physical processes through direct monitoring or control — the Rev. 3 scope, wider than "ICS."

Industrial Control System (ICS)

The classic subset of OT — SCADA, DCS, and PLC-based systems controlling industrial processes — that Rev. 1 and Rev. 2 focused on exclusively.

Overlay

A pre-tailored, specialized set of security controls, control enhancements, and supplemental guidance for a particular type of system or environment — here, OT at a given impact tier.

Security Control Baseline

The starting set of controls associated with a system's impact level (Low, Moderate, High) before any further tailoring is applied.

Tailoring

The documented process of adjusting a control baseline — adding, removing, or modifying controls — to fit an actual environment's constraints.

Compensating Control

An alternative safeguard used when the originally specified control can't be implemented as written — the standard response to OT's patch and legacy constraints.

Risk Management Framework (RMF)

NIST SP 800-37's overall process — categorize, select, implement, assess, authorize, monitor — that SP 800-82's OT risk chapter follows and tailors.

System Security Plan (SSP)

The document that records which controls apply to a system and how they're implemented — where an OT overlay's selections ultimately get written down.

07

Adoption & Practical Use

SP 800-82 occupies a middle ground between CIP-008's legal mandate and ATT&CK for ICS's total lack of enforcement: it is required for U.S. federal agencies through FISMA-driven RMF processes, and voluntarily — but very widely — adopted everywhere else.

Federal Agencies

Required as the OT-specific control reference within FISMA-driven Risk Management Framework processes for federal OT-adjacent systems.

Critical Infrastructure Operators

Adopted voluntarily as a free, vendor-neutral OT security reference — often the first document a new OT security hire is handed.

Auditors & Assessors

Used as a control-mapping reference when assessing an OT environment against the SP 800-53 catalog it tailors.

Vendors & Integrators

Cited directly in RFPs and contracts as the expected control baseline for a delivered OT system, especially in the public sector.

08

Classroom Uses

Overlay Tailoring Drill

Give students five controls from an OT overlay baseline and a fictional plant scenario. For each, they decide: implement as-is, tailor, or compensating control + POA&M — and justify it against OT's safety, availability, and legacy-equipment constraints.

CSF Function Mapping

Take a documented incident (TRITON/TRISIS works well — see the MITRE ATT&CK for ICS page) and map the organization's before/during/after actions onto the six CSF 2.0 functions.

Architecture Sketch

Students draw a reference OT network — IT/OT DMZ, zones, boundary protection — following Pillar 3's guidance, and justify each boundary they drew.

Cross-Framework Bridge

Take the same five overlay controls from the Tailoring Drill and identify which IEC 62443 foundational requirement each one most closely maps to — reinforcing why Lesson 8 treats these frameworks as compatible, not competing.
