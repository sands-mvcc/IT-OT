01

Foundations

ISA/IEC 62443 is a series of standards and technical reports — not a single document — covering the security of Industrial Automation and Control Systems (IACS) across their full lifecycle. It was developed by the ISA99 committee and is jointly published with the International Electrotechnical Commission as IEC 62443, through IEC Technical Committee 65, Working Group 10.

The ISA99 committee formed in the early 2000s as it became clear that IT security standards didn't fit control systems — a firewall rule that's fine for a database server can break a real-time control loop. Growing high-profile incident awareness through the 2000s and 2010s (Stuxnet chief among them) accelerated adoption, and the standard has been continuously expanded and revised since its first parts were published.

62443 is unusual among the Core Frameworks in that its entire structure is organized around three distinct audiences: asset owners who operate the plant, system integrators who design and deploy it, and product suppliers who build the PLCs, HMIs, and network gear that go into it. Each audience gets its own series of the standard, written in language and at a level of abstraction that fits their job.

It's the only framework in this set that is both written specifically for control systems (rather than adapted from IT or general risk management) and formally certifiable — a product, a development process, or an asset owner's security program can all earn a verifiable third-party certification against it. NIST SP 800-82 is guidance; ATT&CK for ICS is descriptive; CIP-008 is a legal mandate for one sector. 62443 is a design standard you can build to and prove you built to.

03

Positioning: IEC 62443 vs. the Other Core Frameworks

NIST SP 800-82 borrows IEC 62443's segmentation ideas rather than replacing them. ATT&CK for ICS documents what attackers actually do to systems that weren't built this way. CIP-008 governs what happens legally once one of those attacks becomes an incident. IEC 62443 is the one framework in the set that answers "how do you design and build this correctly in the first place, and can you prove it?"

04

Security Levels, Requirements & the Four Series

Two pieces of vocabulary — Security Levels and the seven Foundational Requirements — cut across the entire standard and get defined before anything else. Everything after that lives in one of four series, organized by the audience it's written for. The stage colors below reuse this site's existing strand colors: General borrows the orange used for "Foundations," Policies & Procedures borrows the green used for "Operations," System borrows the blue used for "Protocols," and Component borrows the indigo used for "Dev Tools."

Security Levels at a Glance

LevelProtects Against
SL 0No specific protection required
SL 1Casual or coincidental violation
SL 2Intentional violation using simple means, low resources, generic skills, low motivation
SL 3Intentional violation using sophisticated means, moderate resources, IACS-specific skills, moderate motivation
SL 4Intentional violation using sophisticated means, extended resources, IACS-specific skills, high motivation — nation-state level

A zone or component can carry three different SL values: SL-T (Target — what it should achieve), SL-C (Capability — what a product is inherently capable of), and SL-A (Achieved — what's actually in place today). Most real audit findings are a gap between SL-T and SL-A.

The Seven Foundational Requirements

Verifying the identity of users, software processes, and devices before granting access. Covers human authentication, software-process authentication, and device authentication as three distinct requirements.

Enforcing what an already-authenticated identity is actually allowed to do — authorization, session control, and audit logging of use.

Protecting against unauthorized manipulation of hardware, software, configuration, and information — including malware protection and input validation.

Protecting information from unauthorized disclosure, at rest and in transit — the requirement most similar to what IT usually ranks first, here ranked fourth.

Segmenting the network into zones and controlling exactly what crosses a conduit — the requirement that operationalizes zones and conduits themselves.

Detecting security events and responding fast enough to matter for a real-time process — logging, alerting, and incident-response capability.

Keeping the system operating and able to recover — backup, redundancy, and resilience against denial-of-service, reflecting OT's availability-first priority.

Series 1-x — General

Foundational

Defines the core vocabulary — zones, conduits, security levels, and the reference models the rest of the series builds on. This is the part to read first if you're only reading one.

Certification

Describes how certification bodies actually verify conformance to the standard — the process behind an ISASecure certification.

Series 2-x — Policies & Procedures (Asset Owner)

What a plant operator's security management program must actually contain — policy, risk management, and organizational requirements, loosely modeled on the same management-system logic as ISO/IEC 27001.

A process specifically for the reality Lesson 7 already covers — patch windows that don't open on IT's schedule — rather than assuming IT's usual patch cadence applies.

The same kind of program requirements as 2-1, but for the integrators and maintenance contractors an asset owner hires — the standard's answer to third-party risk in OT.

Series 3-x — System (Integrator)

Where zones actually get drawn and assigned a Target Security Level (SL-T) — the methodology that turns a network diagram into a documented security architecture.

Maps specific, testable requirements under each of the seven Foundational Requirements to each Security Level — the part that turns "SL 3" from a label into an actual checklist.

Series 4-x — Component (Product Supplier)

Process requirements for how a vendor builds the product in the first place — secure design, threat modeling, vulnerability handling, and patch delivery over the product's life.

The component-level counterpart to 3-3 — concrete technical requirements a PLC, HMI, or network device must meet to support a given Security Level once it's deployed.

05

Definitions & Scope

These terms recur across every series of the standard — students who are shaky on them will misapply the security-level table above almost immediately.

IACS

Industrial Automation and Control Systems — the full scope of personnel, hardware, software, and policies involved in operating and securing an industrial process.

Zone

A grouping of logical or physical assets that share common security requirements — the unit everything else in the standard is assigned to.

Conduit

The network path connecting two zones, which itself carries its own security requirements — segmentation made explicit and documented rather than assumed.

SL-T / SL-C / SL-A

Target, Capability, and Achieved Security Level — what a zone should reach, what a product can inherently support, and what's actually in place today.

Asset Owner

The organization that operates the IACS day to day — the audience for the 2-x series.

System Integrator

The organization that designs and deploys the IACS on the asset owner's behalf — the audience for the 3-x series.

Product Supplier

The vendor that manufactures the PLCs, HMIs, and network components used in the system — the audience for the 4-x series.

Compensating Countermeasure

An alternative safeguard used when a specified requirement can't be implemented directly — the standard's own version of the compensating-control concept used across CIP-008 and SP 800-82.

07

Certification & Practical Use

Nobody is legally required to adopt 62443 the way NERC entities must adopt CIP-008 — but unlike ATT&CK for ICS, there is something concrete to certify against, and the industry actually uses that option.

Product Certification

Vendors certify individual components against 4-1 (process) and 4-2 (technical requirements) through ISASecure, giving buyers a verifiable claim instead of a marketing one.

Asset Owner Programs

Plants assess or certify their security program against 2-1 and 2-4, turning "we take security seriously" into an auditable statement.

System Integrator Practice

Integrators design to 3-2 and 3-3 even without seeking formal certification, and cite that alignment directly in proposals and RFP responses.

Procurement Language

Buyers write a target Security Level directly into purchase contracts — making 62443 the shared vocabulary a plant and a vendor use to negotiate what "secure enough" actually means.

08

Classroom Uses

Zones & Conduits Redraw

Take the Purdue-model diagram from Lesson 2, redraw it as zones and conduits, and assign a Target Security Level to each zone with a written justification tied to attacker capability, not perceived importance.

FR Coverage Audit

Given a fictional plant's existing controls, students map each control to one of the seven Foundational Requirements and identify which FRs have zero coverage.

Component Certification Review

Students review a fictional vendor datasheet's conformance claims against 4-1/4-2 and decide whether it actually supports the SL-T assigned to the zone it would sit in.

Cross-Framework Bridge

Take the same zone/SL assignments and map them to the SP 800-53 overlay controls from the NIST SP 800-82 page's Overlay Tailoring Drill, showing where the two frameworks reinforce each other.
